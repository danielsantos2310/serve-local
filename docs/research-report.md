# Build booking-first marketplaces, not Deliveroo clones

A Deliveroo-style marketplace for local services is buildable, but only as a booking business that holds the payment, starts with one repeatable category in one area, and checks supply before listing it. Deliveroo itself is a high-volume, thin-margin logistics business: in H1 2025 it kept **27.6p of every pound of gross transaction value (GTV) as revenue** but only **2.5p as adjusted EBITDA**, after **£4.44 per order** of cost of sales, mostly rider pay ([Deliveroo H1 2025 RNS](https://dpd-12774-s3.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/9217/5451/7640/Deliveroo_H1_2025_RNS_vFINAL.pdf)). Services invert that profile. Jobs carry bigger tickets but happen far less often, the supply is self-employed and hard to vet, and the closest comparables are either lead-selling directories that are shrinking or on-demand operators that subsidised supply and then closed or consolidated. On the evidence, cleaning is the best first category. Gas, electrical and childcare carry statutory registration regimes, and the Irish childminder regime is still being reset. **Verdict: yes, it can be built, under five conditions set out below. It fails as a broad directory, as a lead-gen site competing with Checkatrade and MyBuilder, or as a dispatch-driven on-demand service.** The build budget, UK and Irish payment fees and Dublin demand are unverified, so this report cannot price the build.

## How the three sides meet: orders, dispatch and fees

Deliveroo runs three sides. Customers order through the app or website. Restaurants receive each order on a Deliveroo tablet, accept it, and get an estimated rider pick-up time, and they must have the food packed by then ([Deliveroo merchant FAQ](https://merchants.deliveroo.com/faqs)). Deliveroo creates the menu during onboarding, which can take "as little as 7 days" under the same FAQ. Riders are the third side. Deliveroo's dispatch algorithm, called Frank, predicts how long each order will take to prepare from the dish, restaurant location, time of day, number of riders on the road and live order volume. For each online rider it plans the next orders from live location and travel time, and re-plans continuously, aiming to get the rider to the restaurant near collection time ([Deliveroo rider support: offers](https://riders.deliveroo.co.uk/en/support/orders/how-and-why-am-i-offered-specific-orders)). This matters because the algorithm's job is to minimise idle time at restaurants, and that is the core efficiency of the whole model.

Riders are self-employed. Each order carries its own fee, shown before acceptance, based primarily on the road distance and estimated time to complete it. Other inputs include rider location at offer time, stacked pick-ups, time of day, how busy the area is, boosts and vehicle type. Acceptance history, tenure and past completion times do not affect the fee, and riders are paid only for completed orders ([Deliveroo UK rider support: fees](https://riders.deliveroo.co.uk/en/support/fees/how-are-fees-calculated)). Under a GMB agreement reviewed annually, UK riders have a pay floor of **£12.30 per hour plus vehicle costs for 2025–2026**, measured from acceptance to delivery. A 20-minute order therefore pays at least £4.10 plus vehicle costs. Tips pass through in full.

Restaurants pay for the service through a commission that Deliveroo does not publish in its FAQ. A third-party guide puts typical commission at **about 25% to 35% of order value**, but it cites no source and calls itself "a rough guide for 2026" ([DineOpen](https://www.dineopen.com/blog/deliveroo-vs-just-eat-vs-uber-eats-commission-2026)). Restaurants are paid weekly, with periods running Monday to Sunday and payment every Thursday in the UK. Quality is managed through a monthly **Value Score**, where high scores bring more app visibility and low scores bring site limitations. Restaurants can choose a fulfilment model: standard Deliveroo riders, Marketplace+ (their own riders with a set radius), Deliveroo Express (riders fulfil orders placed through the restaurant's own website or app), or Editions delivery-only kitchens, which the FAQ says carry "a simple single percentage commission" ([Deliveroo merchant FAQ](https://merchants.deliveroo.com/faqs)).

Customers pay a basket-based service fee, which the current comms page sets at **5% of basket value with a 49p minimum**, plus distance-based delivery fees. Deliveroo Plus is the subscription. The comms page says the monthly fee has been cut from £11.49 to **£7.99** and that Plus members pay no more than 99p in service fees per order. A help page gives a different cap, £1.49 for Plus Gold. Neither page is dated, so the current customer fee schedule is unconfirmed ([Deliveroo comms page](https://deliveroo.co.uk/comms/v3qwwb8kvj); [Deliveroo help: new Plus proposition](https://help.deliveroo.com/en/articles/9230820-introducing-our-new-plus-proposition)). Plus Gold also gives a £5 credit when an order arrives late, and Diamond gives full order-value credit when it is more than 10 minutes late. Those credits are the main quality lever on the customer side, and their cost is not disclosed.

## Where the money goes: 27.6p revenue, 2.5p profit

The H1 2025 interim report is the last full set of standalone results Deliveroo published. Orders rose **8% to 147.0 million**, GTV rose **9% (constant currency) to £3,788.7 million**, and revenue reached **£1,046.6 million**. Adjusted EBITDA was **£96.3 million**, or 2.5% of GTV, up from 1.9% a year earlier. The statutory result was a **£19.2 million loss**, driven by £48.8 million of exceptional items, mostly DoorDash-related fees and costs ([Deliveroo H1 2025 RNS](https://dpd-12774-s3.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/9217/5451/7640/Deliveroo_H1_2025_RNS_vFINAL.pdf)). The full-year picture is thinner still: FY2024 was the first full year of statutory profit, at **£2.9 million**, on GTV of £7.4 billion ([CityAM](https://www.cityam.com/?p=2314578)).

The revenue take rate of 27.6% is an aggregate across restaurant commission, consumer fees and other income, so it cannot be read as the restaurant's commission rate. Each order earns roughly 66p of adjusted profit, so a small rise in orders per rider or per area moves the result far more than a price change does. Deliveroo was valued at roughly **US$3.9 billion** in the headline reports of DoorDash's takeover, which **completed on 2 October 2025** and made Deliveroo a DoorDash subsidiary ([DoorDash release](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20251002060133/en/DoorDash-Completes-Acquisition-of-Deliveroo); [Hurriyet Daily News headline](https://hurriyetdailynews.com/doordash-agrees-3-9-billion-takeover-of-deliveroo-208852)). The value rests on headlines only. DoorDash's release gives no figure, and no post-acquisition Deliveroo results were found.

## Competitors: lead sellers shrink, bookers grow, instant operators burn cash

Local services platforms fall into three models. Lead-generation marketplaces sell a homeowner's enquiry to several tradespeople. Booking marketplaces take a cut of each completed job. On-demand operators own the supply, the dispatch and the job itself. The Deliveroo model maps most closely onto the last two, because the platform controls payment and dispatch. Lead-gen platforms do not hold the transaction and so cannot easily earn a per-job take.

The lead-gen segment is shrinking. **Angi**, the largest listed US comparable, reported Q2 2026 revenue of **$248.0 million, down 11%**, with active US pros down **17% to about 106,000** and a membership subscription line of only **$5.7 million**. The company attributes the decline partly to a "homeowner choice transition" introduced in January 2025, and it cut about **350 roles in January 2026** ([Angi Q2 2026 10-Q](https://angihomeservices.gcs-web.com/static-files/8e885886-aac9-4cf3-a4a5-0533bd057de3)). Checkatrade's primary pricing page shows a subscription model rather than commission: a free tier, an Approved tier at £30 per month, and a Growth tier from £59 per month that buys a yearly lead volume ([Checkatrade pricing](https://join.checkatrade.com/pricing); undated page). Its UK rivals MyBuilder and Checkatrade are described in a competitor blog as charging £10 to £50 per lead and "several thousand pounds per year" respectively. That blog is dated March 2025 and cites no sources, so the Checkatrade figure conflicts with the primary pricing page and should be ignored in favour of it ([BookaBuilderUK](https://bookabuilderuk.com/blog/trade-site-comparisons)).

The booking segment has the strongest precedent. **TaskRabbit** opened in London in November 2013 with about 50 "Taskers" across cleaning, errands and handyman work, after surveying about 2,000 consumers to choose categories. Its launch claims, made by the company, were that supply was never a problem and that 75% of business came from word of mouth ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2013/11/20/taskrabbit-takes-its-errands-marketplace-to-the-uk-for-its-first-move-outside-the-u-s)). TaskRabbit charges customers a service fee "a percentage of the total price", but the support page gives no rate ([TaskRabbit support](https://support.taskrabbit.com/hc/en-gb/articles/204411610)). **Rover**, a recurring pet-care booking marketplace, charges pet owners 25% on top of the sitter's rate plus an 11% booking fee, and sitters pay nothing. My arithmetic from those stated fees puts the platform's take at about 28% of what the owner pays ([Rover support](https://support.rover.com/hc/en-us/articles/12439190554132), undated). Blackstone took Rover private for about **$2.3 billion**, completed in February 2024 ([Blackstone](https://www.blackstone.com/news/press/blackstone-completes-acquisition-of-rover/)). **Urban Company**, the Indian home-services leader, reports **FY26 revenue of ₹1,555.5 crore, up 35.9%**, but a consolidated net loss of ₹234.8 crore, according to secondary sources ([Scanx](https://scanx.trade/stock-market-news/companies/urban-company-posts-consolidated-net-loss-of-234-81-crore-in-fy26-as-revenue-surges-35-93-to-1-555-54-crore/39786926); search snippet). Its commissions and fees equalled **28.3% of professionals' gross earnings** in the nine months to December 2025, a figure its source says should not be read as the overall take rate ([Inc42, Sept 2026](https://inc42.com/features/inside-urban-companys-marketplace-model-what-it-keeps-from-every-booking/)). Management said pros need about ₹130–160 an hour to earn sustainably, and that consumer pricing would need to reach about ₹200 an hour. Urban Company's Saudi subsidiary was dissolved and the business moved into a joint venture, with no financial reason given ([Enterprise AM, June 2026](https://enterpriseam.com/mena-india/2026/06/03/urban-companys-saudi-ops-move-to-yammak-following-the-closure-of-its-subsidiary/)).

The instant segment has the worst record. **Pync**, an instant house-help operator in Bengaluru, shut on **15 January 2026** after peaking at 25,000+ households, about 5,000 daily orders and 1,000+ professionals. It cited intensifying competition in 10-minute house help, and its co-founders joined rival **Snabbit** ([Indian Startup News, Jan 2026](https://indianstartupnews.com/news/quick-home-services-startup-pync-shuts-down-founders-join-rival-snabbit-11000365)). Snabbit then closed a **$56 million Series D in April 2026** ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/04/27/indias-snabbit-closes-56m-round-as-investor-interest-heats-up-in-on-demand-home-services/)). A secondary headline reports that Snabbit spent ₹16 for every ₹1 of operating revenue in FY26, which would signal heavy subsidy, but the article was not read. In the US, **Homejoy** closed in 2015 ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2015/07/17/homejoy-closing-cleaning-google/)), and **Handy** raised $50 million the same year ([Fortune](https://dc.fortune.com/2015/11/02/handy-50-million/)). Taskbob and DoorMint are both reported as shut, with dates unconfirmed ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/flash-feed/taskbob-shutdown/); [Inc42](https://inc42.com/flash-feed/doormint-shutdown)).

Two patterns emerge. Instant operators that subsidise both sides have burned cash and lost to better-funded or consolidated rivals. Lead-gen directories are losing the pros who make them valuable. The surviving model is booking, with payment control, density and recurring product attachments. Urban Company's clearest retention signal is product attach: about **75% of early water-purifier customers** renewed filters through the platform, though the figure is unattributed ([Inc42](https://inc42.com/features/inside-urban-companys-marketplace-model-what-it-keeps-from-every-booking/)). A separate, undated secondary source claims **82% repeat users** with no definition ([Finology](https://ticker.finology.in/discover/market-update/urban-company-ipo-analysis)). Neither figure should be used for planning.

## Regulation: registers, vetting and worker status set the limits

Regulation decides which categories a marketplace can list on day one. Gas is the highest-harm category. The Gas Safe Register says it takes complaints about "illegal gas workers" and says "Carbon monoxide poisoning is one of the risks of unsafe gas work" ([Gas Safe Register](https://www.gassaferegister.co.uk)). The registration rules and landlord duties were not retrieved, so the research cannot say exactly what a marketplace must check. Electrical work has the clearest route in both countries. In England, **Approved Document P** lets registered competent-person installers certify their own work, and other installers must use a registered third-party certifier ([Planning Portal](https://www.planningportal.co.uk/applications/building-control-applications/building-control/approved-documents/part-p-electrical-safety)). In the Republic of Ireland, **Safe Electric**, run by RECI for the Commission for Regulation of Utilities, is a legal requirement for anyone doing electrical work in a domestic setting, with minor works excluded. Applicants with foreign qualifications must have them recognised first, and complete applications take **15 working days** to process ([gov.ie](https://www.gov.ie/en/service/e6678-register-as-an-electrician)). Plumbing has no licensing body in either country according to the notes, which makes it a trust-and-quality problem rather than a licensing one. Plumbing rules were not searched.

Childcare has the most fragile rules. In England, childminders must register with Ofsted, but the nanny rules depend on the arrangement, and the gov.uk pages that set them out could not be retrieved ([gov.uk, nannies](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/childminders-and-childcare-providers-register-with-ofsted/nannies-registration)). In Ireland, **Tusla childminder registration is not yet mandatory**. An Irish Examiner report dated 19 October 2026 says the deadline is being extended to around **September 2027**, with a review due at the end of October 2026. By the end of August 2026 only **202 of an estimated 13,000 childminders** had registered, and 878 had completed pre-registration training ([Irish Examiner](https://www.irishexaminer.com/news/politics/arid-41926148.html)). The article date and the 2027 date come from a fetch summary and should be rechecked against the article. Any Irish childcare product would need to switch rules on a date, not assume a fixed regime.

Worker status is the risk that matters most for a platform that dispatches or sets terms. The UK Supreme Court's **Uber v Aslam** ruling held that drivers were "workers", using control factors rather than the written contract. Those factors included Uber setting fares, imposing terms, restricting job acceptance through a log-off penalty, and controlling service through ratings ([UK Supreme Court press summary](https://supremecourt.uk/uploads/uksc_2019_0029_press_summary_573bbeb959.pdf)). Deliveroo's own case went the other way on a narrower question. In **IWGB v CAC (21 November 2023)** the Supreme Court held that riders were not in an employment relationship for union-recognition purposes, largely because the contract gives riders a "broad, virtually unfettered right" to send a substitute ([UKSC press summary](https://mta-sts.supremecourt.uk/cases/docs/uksc-2021-0155-press-summary.pdf)). The substitution right was the decisive fact. A services marketplace that lets pros set prices, decline work and send substitutes sits on the Deliveroo side of that line. One that imposes rates, penalises declines and uses ratings to discipline pros looks more like Uber. The EU Platform Work Directive adds a **rebuttable presumption of employment** for platform workers, with national transposition due by **2 December 2026**. Irish transposition is unconfirmed ([Remote Work Europe](https://remoteworkeurope.eu/news/2026/eu-platform-work-directive-transposition/)). The UK Employment Rights Act 2025 has received Royal Assent, but its implementation timetable and effect on classification were not confirmed.

Consumer law adds a compliance duty. Since **6 April 2025**, the DMCC Act requires businesses and platforms to "take steps to prevent and remove" fake reviews, and the platform hosting a review is accountable for it ([gov.uk](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/fake-reviews-and-sneaky-hidden-fees-banned-once-and-for-all)). Verified-job reviews, where only customers who paid through the platform can review, are the obvious control. CMA fine levels were not confirmed from a fetched source. Insurance, dispute resolution and Irish consumer law were not researched.

## Verdict: buildable, but only as a booking-first, narrow-start business

The answer to the second question is yes, a Deliveroo-style marketplace for local services can be built and can work, but "Deliveroo-style" must mean the payment and trust layer, not the dispatch engine. The conditions below come from the evidence above, and each rules out a version of the idea that has failed or is legally exposed.

| Condition | Evidence | What it rules out |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The platform holds the payment from day one | Thumbtack moved from subscriptions to lead fees because it could not track each provider's revenue, and the author argues commission needs transaction control ([Sharetribe Academy, undated](https://www.sharetribe.com/academy/why-thumbtack-succeeded/)). Rover's fee structure depends on owning payment. | Pure lead-gen directories with no take rate |
| 2. One recurring, low-ticket category in one area first | TaskRabbit launched with cleaning among its categories. Thumbtack's author says single-bid experiences drove customers away, so density matters more than breadth. Repeat use in services depends on category, not daily frequency. | Launching every trade in every city at once |
| 3. Booking, not on-demand dispatch | Pync shut in January 2026, Snabbit's reported cost ratio signals heavy subsidy, and Homejoy closed in 2015. | Subsidised instant service and Deliveroo-style dispatch |
| 4. Verified supply before listing | Safe Electric is mandatory for domestic electrical work in Ireland. Gas Safe handles complaints about unregistered gas workers. Irish childminders are mostly unregistered. Fake reviews are illegal. | Listing unchecked gas, electrical or childcare workers |
| 5. Pros set their own price and can decline | The Uber control factors and the Deliveroo substitution right are the two classification tests the research found. | Platform-set rates and penalties for declining jobs |

Condition 5 creates a real tension. The feature that makes Deliveroo efficient, algorithmic dispatch that routes work to the nearest rider, is the same feature that drives classification risk. A services marketplace can keep the efficiency by offering matching and suggested slots while leaving the pro free to decline and set price, but it gives up some of the density engine that makes Deliveroo's margin work. The trade-off is real and cannot be avoided by drafting.

The market case is less clear than the product case. The ONS reports average weekly household spending of **£676.60** for the UK, with housing and fuel the largest category at 18% ([ONS](https://www.ons.gov.uk/peoplepopulationandcommunity/personalandhouseholdfinances/expenditure/bulletins/familyspendingintheuk/latest)). The Irish CSO reports **€1,007.47** a week for households in 2022–23 ([CSO](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-hbs/householdbudgetsurvey2022-2023/keyfindings/)). Neither splits out repairs, cleaning or childcare. Demand for childcare in Ireland is clear: **nearly 46,000 children under three** were on waiting lists in August 2026, up 16% in a year ([Irish Examiner](https://www.irishexaminer.com/news/arid-41900833.html)). Childminding is a different market from the formal childcare on those lists, and the research did not size it. UK childcare costs are about **£57–£70 a week** for 25 hours in England after government funding ([Coram Childcare Survey 2025](https://coram.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/Childcare-Survey-2025.pdf)). No Dublin-specific count of tradespeople, cleaners or childminders was verified.

## A realistic build path for the UK and Dublin

The path below is my recommendation, not a sourced plan. The research found no verified build cost, team size or timeline, so each phase needs its own costing.

Start with demand before code. Run a manual cleaning service in one Dublin or London area: take bookings by form, match the work by hand, and measure whether customers rebook. The evidence says the gate is repeat use. TaskRabbit's supply claims were company statements at launch, and Thumbtack's author says customers who received no bid did not return, so the test should measure bids per job and rebooking, not sign-ups. Verify each cleaner's identity and right to work by hand at this stage, which also tests how much friction vetting adds.

Next, build a booking MVP that holds the money. Its core is a provider profile, a bookable slot, a payment taken at booking, a payout to the provider, identity checks, messaging and reviews limited to paid jobs. Stripe Connect is the obvious payment layer, but only US pricing was read: **$2 per monthly active account**, payouts at **0.25% plus 25 cents**, and card processing from **2.9% plus 30 cents** ([Stripe Connect pricing](https://stripe.com/connect/pricing)). Because Stripe charges per active account, the model should count providers who take jobs each month, not registered ones. UK and Irish rates, identity-check costs and build options (no-code, white-label or custom) were not verified. Sharetribe is named in the sources as a white-label option, but its pricing was not read.

Add a second category only when the first one's repeat rate is known. Recurring maintenance and handyman work is the natural extension, and gas and electrical should come in only once the platform can check the relevant register before listing, Gas Safe for gas and Safe Electric or Part P routes for electrical. Childcare in Ireland should wait until the Tusla regime is final. The strongest case there is demand, but the regulatory ground is still moving, and the sources reviewed did not size it. Take rates should start as a booking fee on the Rover model, but no UK take rate for a services marketplace was verified, so the figure must be tested rather than assumed.

Track four numbers from the start: repeat rate by category, active providers per month, share of requests that get a bid or a booking, and take rate per completed job. These are the figures that decide whether to expand, and the research found no benchmark for any of them in UK or Irish services.

## Conclusion: the moat is trust and payment, not density

The most useful finding is that Deliveroo's economics do not transfer as a whole. Deliveroo earns about 66p of adjusted profit per order because it runs a dense, algorithm-driven network of self-employed riders. Services do not produce that density. A cleaner or a plumber serves a household a few times a year, so the business gets its density within a neighbourhood and its repeat use within a category. What does transfer is the payment and trust layer: taking the money, verifying the people, and tying reviews to paid work. That layer is also where the legal and regulatory duties sit, from registration checks to fake-review removal.

The regulatory finding reframes the product choice. The more a marketplace dispatches and sets terms, the more it resembles Deliveroo's riders in the Uber line of cases, and the weaker its position on worker status becomes. A booking-first platform that lets providers set prices and decline work is safer legally, but it gives up the dispatch efficiency that made Deliveroo profitable. That trade-off, not the technology, is the decision a founder has to make first, and no amount of drafting removes it.
